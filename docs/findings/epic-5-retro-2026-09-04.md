## 2. Post-run observations

**1. The boundary a security story defends was not the boundary it was built on.** 5.6's whole design is a `z.strictObject` with nowhere to write an assertion — and that schema works. Round 4 of its review found the hole was in the **candidate rule** instead: it offered the adapter any probe with an unsatisfied assertion, including a plain product failure (the title says `Orders` where the contract requires `Organizations`). Asking a provider for new navigation until an **unchanged** assertion passes elsewhere is laundering a real failure, and **the schema is never engaged, because nothing it guards is altered**. *Why no story caught it:* every test asked whether an illegal payload was refused; none asked which failures were offered in the first place. *Fix:* the trigger is now one structured signal (`execError.reason === 'step-target-missing'`) and every `fail` is excluded. **The feature does materially less than AC1's framing implies, and that is the correct outcome.**

**2. The owner's D12 decision was load-bearing, and my recommendation was wrong.** I found that AC1's own worked example — a relabelled button the probe *clicks* — produced an `execError` and was therefore unadaptable, and I recommended accepting the narrower scope. The owner overruled me and required it closed inside 5.6 (a structured reason on `ProbeExecError`). Observation 1 is why that mattered: **without that structured signal the only candidates available were the assertion-based ones — the unsafe half.** Closing D12 did not merely widen the story; it removed the reason its rule was wrong. Recorded here because a supervisor's recommendations should be scored, not only issued.

**3. Two stories derived the same prompt-assembly defects independently, hours apart.** 5.5 (round 2) and 5.6 (round 13) each found that bounding the prompt cut off its tail — where the response shape and the valid-ids rule live, so the runs with the most to say sent a prompt that never states what to reply with — and each found that the contract `statement` travels **verbatim from the frozen contract** with no earlier boundary redacting it. *Why no story caught it:* there is no shared prompt-assembly helper; `src/authoring/explain.ts` and 5.6's adaptation prompt are separate code that made the same two mistakes. *Fix:* none applied — deliberately, since neither story owned the other's module. **Action item e5-A.**

**4. Overclaims are a defect class the project's own tooling cannot catch.** Four of 5.6's nineteen findings, and at least one of 5.5's, were sentences true of the case in front of the author and false in general: *"depcruise proves the placement"*, *"the adapted attempt list has exactly one attempt"* (false once `retries.browser` is set), *"everything sent is redacted"* (twice). The code was right and the prose was wrong. **Planting catches vacuous guards; it catches nothing here**, because there is no test to make red. Reviewers caught all of them.

**5. One of those overclaims was Epic 4 lesson 5, violated by an agent quoting Epic 4 lesson 5.** 5.6's author argued the contract statement needed no redaction *partly because 5.5's explainer sends it on the same footing* — it does not; `explain.ts` clips it through `redactText` and says why. He characterised a **merged** peer's code from memory and built a **security** decision on it, while the sentence *"describe your own diff; point at, but do not characterise, anyone else's"* was in his own PR body. He found it himself and corrected code, header and body. *Fix:* the lesson needs teeth beyond restatement — see e5-B.

**6. `src/authoring/**` has no dependency-cruiser rule.** `ingest`, `pipeline` and `report` each have one; `authoring` appears in `.dependency-cruiser.cjs` only inside other rules' prose. Verified by me directly after 5.5's author reported planting `authoring → infra` and watching depcruise pass. The layer now holds `plan.ts`, `amend.ts`, `explain.ts` and 5.6's adaptation modules — four modules and no enforcement, in the layer that composes every provider call. Neither agent authored a rule, correctly: both specs put it out of scope, and a layer boundary drawn late by whoever noticed last is how it gets drawn wrong. **Action item e5-C.**

**7. The cross-story defect class Epic 5 predicted for itself arrived three times.** Wave 2's journal named `routeWebSocket` — arriving in Playwright 1.48 while 5.1 honours whatever version a project pinned — as *"the first defect in the epic that no single story's tests could have caught"*, and warned wave 3 to expect more. Wave 3 produced two: the adapted re-execution feeding 5.4's flake rule (below), and 5.6's own probe-id collision, where ids are unique **within a criterion** but the adaptation stage tracked them globally, so a namesake that flaked to a pass could replace an **unrelated** criterion's result with nothing in the record to explain it.

**8. A supervisor reading merged code found a seam neither author could see from inside.** I raised the flake seam from `criterion-result.ts:441-443` and `probes.ts:299-302` before any run had shown it: an adapted re-execution lands in the original probe's attempt array by construction, and `[fail with old mechanics, pass with new]` is exactly the input that yields `flaky: true`. 5.6's author confirmed he had not handled it and that **it would have shipped green**, because nothing he had written yet looked at the attempt list. *Fix:* the re-execution derives from its own fresh attempt list, so `flaky` is structurally false rather than filtered. Both authors' vocabularies had already agreed; the mechanical question sat between the two agreements.

**9. Stage 4 removed the bottleneck Epic 4 measured, and introduced a reporting duty.** Epic 4 lost ~7h43m of critical path to four ready PRs waiting overnight for a merge click. In Epic 5 the longest gap between a supervisor verdict and its merge was under a minute. The cost is that **the owner never saw six PRs**, so anything living only in a PR body reached nobody — wave 2's journal already recorded escalating the unredacted-trace finding for exactly this reason, and I carried three findings out of #59 and five out of #60 into the window and this document for the same one.

## 3. What we learned

- **A structural guard proves only what its inputs let it see.** 5.6's schema was correct and complete, and the story was still unsafe until the *selection* of what reached it was fixed. Ask what is offered to a boundary, not only what it refuses.
- **Two agreements can both hold and still leave a gap between them.** Vocabulary was settled at intent-sync; the mechanical question — does the re-executed attempt enter the array — belonged to neither agreement.
- **Prose is unverified code.** Four overclaims survived every gate this project has. The mitigations that work are a reviewer and an author re-reading their own work after the review that was supposed to be final; two of the epic's most serious findings came from the latter.
- **A supervisor's escalation should be scored.** D12 is the second time in two epics that reading merged code adjacently to a story found something the story could not, and the first time the owner's answer against my recommendation is what made a story safe.

## 4. What went well

- **Six stories, six `ready` verdicts, zero rework after merge.** Every gate I reported was one I ran myself, in a throwaway worktree rather than in a live agent's tree — the mitigation e3-B has been asking for since Epic 3, and this is the first epic where the supervisor did not repeat that mistake.
- **The wave plan held exactly**, three times, with no cross-wave contention and one rebase per wave-3 agent.
- **Agents held their own merges.** Wave 2's journal records 5.2's author stopping her supervisor's merge after finding a P2 the supervisor had already passed. Under Stage 4 an agent willing to do that is load-bearing.
- **Both wave-3 authors dismissed findings with written reasoning and none silently**, including one dismissal I independently verified before accepting (5.5's refusal to bump `jsonReport`: `flakiness` has shipped unconditionally since 5.4 with the version at 1, so the property predates the story and is broader than it).
- **The renderer split across four stories held with zero edits between them**, and `src/report/json.ts` is still one line after six stories touched the document it serialises.

## 5. Technical debt carried forward

- No shared prompt-assembly helper (observation 3).
- No dependency-cruiser rule for `src/authoring/**` (observation 6).
- `.strict()` on the persisted envelope versus "evolve by adding optional keys": an older build reading a newer document rejects it as *malformed* while `schemaVersion` still reads 1. Four registry keys are affected; it predates this epic. ADR-level.
- `report --explain` is unimplemented because a merged guard (`report.test.ts:399-412`, rationale Q52 — *"no provider"*) forbids it while the 5.5 spec's clarifications assume it. Two agents reached that conclusion independently. Narrowing the guard is an architecture decision.
- The unredacted Playwright trace (wave 2, owner decided to merge as-is and decide later).
- 5.6 excludes path drift and every assertion-target miss. Safety-motivated and correct, but it means FR-18's coverage is narrower than the FR reads.

## 10. Three waves plus Stage 4 — the short answer

**It answered e4-A, and the answer is yes.** Epic 4's three sequential cohorts eliminated subscription freezes but lost ~7h43m of critical path to merge-click latency. Epic 5 kept the sequential structure, added a supervisor permitted to merge story PRs into the epic, and that latency went to under a minute — six times. Supervised time was 12h37m for six stories against Epic 4's 20h54m for seven.

**The price is real and it is a reporting price, not a safety one.** The gate's three conditions (fresh `codex_review`, fresh `security_check`, a `clean` rebase verdict) held every time and refused nothing improperly; wave 2's journal records the one case where they deadlocked on `touched` and the owner fixed the harness mid-wave. What changed is that **the owner never read a story PR this epic**, so every finding that would once have surfaced at a merge click had to be carried out deliberately — by the supervisor, into the window, the reports file, the journal and this document. Where that discipline slipped, nothing would have caught it.

**One recommendation for Epic 6.** Stage 4 works, and it makes the supervisor's per-PR verdict the last independent read of the code. That verdict is worth more when the supervisor reproduces the gates rather than reading a summary of them — which costs about ten minutes per PR in a throwaway worktree, and caught nothing this epic. It caught nothing because both authors were accurate; the cost of finding that out is the point.
